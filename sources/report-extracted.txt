23 OCT 2026  |  RESEARCH SYNTHESIS 1
RESEARCH LANDSCAPE / TECHNICAL BLUEPRINT
Tactile World Models, Texture
Simulation & Whole-Body
Somatosensation
A comprehensive synthesis of our discussion: what a robot should feel, how to
simulate it, what researchers have built, and a feasible route toward physically
grounded touch for Physical AI.
Prepared 23 October 2026 | Covers discussion from 29 September to 23 October 2026
Includes: material representations and contact physics; 2026 tactile world models; visual-tactile simulation; differentiable
mechanics; whole-body artificial skin; datasets and tactile foundation models; research hypotheses; phased experiments;
and an annotated reading catalog.
CENTRAL QUESTION
Can an embodied AI system learn a reusable physical description of surface texture and
contact, simulate what different bodies and sensors would feel, and use those predictions
to act reliably in the real world?
Evidence convention: Published work and reported experimental results are distinguished from design proposals and hypotheses.
Example material parameter values are illustrative, not calibrated measurements. Source links appear in the numbered literature
catalog.

1. Executive synthesis
Short answer: Tactile world models already exist. They predict future tactile or visuotactile observations from recent
sensor readings, actions and sometimes force/torque signals. Examples are SPOTS, TacForeSight, TACO, FeelWorld and
ContactWorld [39] [40] [41] [42] [43]. Their published demonstrations are mostly task-specific or sensor-specific; none
establishes a universal, physically calibrated, whole-body tactile world.
A conventional robot simulator already models rigid-body geometry, inertia, contact, friction and sometimes deformable
mechanics. But a single friction coefficient or soft-contact parameter does not reproduce the fine spatial and temporal
signals of fabric, wood grain, skin or rubber sliding over a fingertip. Optical tactile simulators can generate sophisticated
sensor images; electronic skins sense distributed contact; tactile encoders learn compact representations. These
advances solve different parts of the problem.
Proposed long-term objective: Build a physically grounded somatosensory world model in which object materials and
body sensors are modeled separately, a contact engine outputs pressure/shear/deformation/slip/vibration/heat, and a
learned predictor improves dynamics forecasts and action selection. Whole-body touch is a long-term integration goal,
not a prerequisite for a credible first experiment.

The safest concise project name is physically grounded tactile world model for the predictive component, with a
tactile-aware physics and sensor simulation layer underneath it. The eventual full-body system can reasonably be
described as a somatosensory world model.
Why vision alone is not enough
Two surfaces can look identical yet differ in friction, stiffness, viscoelasticity, temperature and wear. Touch supplies
latent information that determines whether a grasp slips, a surface scratches, an insertion jams or an object deforms.
SPOTS specifically studies settings with visually similar objects but different physical properties and finds tactile signals
are most valuable when the physical differences are visually ambiguous [39].
Why “a 3D model plus friction” is not enough
Traditional simulation does include forces and friction; it is inaccurate to say world models or physics engines have only
3D meshes. The limitation is that many robotics pipelines do not render sensor-realistic, high-frequency, spatially
distributed tactile experiences. A friction coefficient can help predict gross sliding; it cannot by itself recreate the woven
microtexture of denim or the vibration spectrum of brushed metal.
3. What to represent in a touchable physical world
A touchable object needs more than a texture image. A useful physical description has geometry at several scales; local
mechanical, tribological and thermal properties; and uncertainty about those properties. The robot also has its own
compliant skin and sensor dynamics. These are distinct entities.

What actually drives a simulated sensor.
A basic material record might contain a mean friction coefficient and bulk stiffness. That describes a material category,
not a complete tactile texture. For a surface parameterized by local coordinates (u,v), propose a spatial material field:
M(u,v) = [ h(u,v), μs(u,v), μd(u,v), E(u,v), ν(u,v), η(u,v), A(u,v), kth(u,v), ... ]
Here h is microgeometry height; μ are friction parameters; E and ν describe elasticity; η is damping/viscoelastic
behavior; A is adhesion; and kth is a thermal parameter. A richer implementation allows orientation, strain, speed,
normal load, time, wear and environmental conditions to affect those values.
Material is not sensation
The same object produces different signals when pressed, slid or tapped. Therefore, the sensation is a function of
material + surface geometry + action + contact physics + the sensing body:
Tsensor(t) = Rendersensor( Contact( M, G, Body, a(0:t) ) )
Example tactile outputs range from an optical GelSight RGB/deformation image to a sampled pressure/taxel array or
even a three-channel 128 × 128 normal/shear-force grid (the latter is an illustrative output design, not a universal
sensor standard). A smooth glass surface is nearly flat, comparatively stiff and often frictionally uniform at the
resolution of a coarse model. Denim has oriented weave microgeometry and direction-dependent interactions. Rubber
may have strong compliance, adhesion and rate-dependent hysteresis. These are qualitative examples; numerical
values must come from measurement.
Illustrative tactile material asset
Denim_12oz_014.tactile (hypothetical file schema): spatial height/normal maps; weave period and direction; local
friction parameters with uncertainty; normal/shear compliance; mechanical damping; temperature-dependent
properties; calibration provenance; supported scales, ranges of loading/speed, and validation errors. Specific values
such as a 0.4 mm period or friction 0.52, mentioned in our earlier discussion, were illustrative only, not empirical
estimates for denim.
Other example material assets discussed: oak wood, brushed aluminum, corrugated cardboard, cotton, silicone rubber,
smooth glass and human fingertip/forearm skin. Each would require standardized characterization before becoming an
interchangeable simulation asset.

4. How texture becomes a tactile signal
The critical physical chain is: surface topography and material properties → contact mechanics →
pressure/shear/slip/vibration/heat → sensor output. Fine tactile texture is typically dynamic: sliding excites frictional
forces and vibrations, so surface roughness alone does not specify the experience.
Normal and tangential contact
A point-contact approximation may report only an aggregate normal force. A touch-aware simulator should ideally
produce a distributed contact patch:
C(u,v,t) = [ Pn, τx, τy, δ, vslip, T, q, ... ](u,v,t)
Pn is pressure; τ are tangential tractions; δ is deformation; vslip local sliding velocity; T is temperature; q heat flux or
related signal. The sensor renderer can then integrate or filter C into pixels, taxels, magnetic readings or strain gauge
signals.
Slip is more than a binary label
Contact may transition from sticking to partial slip and then gross sliding. Incipient slip can begin at the edge of a
contact patch before the object visibly moves. Realistic simulation may need friction that depends on speed, load,
surface condition and direction. For texture, compare temporal force fluctuations and vibration spectra across scanning
speeds, not just scalar friction coefficients.
Human texture perception and vibration
Human-inspired research highlights the joint role of slowly varying deformation and fast vibration. One 2023
sensory-system paper measures both spatial pressure and high-frequency responses and demonstrates texture
recognition [20]. Related material-identification work studies thermal and vibration cues, cross-modal texture reasoning
and triboelectric effects [21] [22] [23] [24] [25]. These are useful for selecting which physical quantities to measure, but
recognition accuracy alone does not establish that a simulator can synthesize touch.
The “PBR for touch” analogy
Visual physically based rendering (PBR) commonly uses maps such as albedo, normal and roughness. The proposed
analogy is a standardized physically based tactile material encoding microgeometry, friction, compliance, damping,
adhesion, thermal behavior and uncertainty. Unlike ordinary visual textures, tactile assets must be specified with units,
measurement protocols and validity ranges. The target is not a new file extension alone; it is a material representation
whose predictions survive changes in action and sensor.

Optical tactile rendering: TACTO, GelSight in Gazebo, Taxim, Tactile Gym 2.0
TACTO demonstrates fast configurable rendering for high-resolution optical tactile sensing [4]. GelSight-in-Gazebo work
introduces synthetic GelSight-style contact images in conventional robotics simulation [5]. Taxim uses an
example-based model, including optical response and elastomer/marker displacement, to better reproduce GelSight
observations [6]. Tactile Gym 2.0 extends optical tactile learning and sim-to-real evaluation across tactile sensor
designs [7].
These systems are crucial, but optical realism and physical validity are separate questions. A generated tactile RGB
image may look plausible while encoding inaccurate friction, microscopic shear or vibration. Sim-to-real validation
should compare measured physical outcomes, not only image similarity.
TacSL and newer GPU pipelines
TacSL integrates accelerated visuotactile simulation and robot-learning workflows in a GPU ecosystem [8]. Isaac Lab’s
experimental visuotactile module can calculate normal/shear force fields with an elastomer response [49]. These
systems may make large-scale policy training practical, but the user’s proposed ambition still requires broader material
parameterization, better experimental calibration and whole-body coverage.
HydroelasticTouch and Drake
Hydroelastic formulations replace one isolated contact point with a surface carrying pressure information.
HydroelasticTouch applies this principle to tactile rendering [9], while Drake documents compliant/hydroelastic contact
mechanics [10]. NVIDIA’s emerging hydroelastic contact tools pursue similar distributed contact primitives [50]. Spatial
contact matters because fingertip and skin observations depend on where forces are applied, not only their total
magnitude.
High-fidelity elastomer mechanics
FEM-based approaches can model the elastic skin geometry, marker movement and internal stress much more
explicitly, though at a significant computational cost. The 2026 marker-level 3D deformation study exemplifies
physics-rich visuotactile sensor modeling [11]. This makes it valuable as a reference simulator to calibrate faster tactile
approximations.

Simulation backends in the discussion
MuJoCo is a strong starting point for control and contact-rich robotics [12]. Drake offers well-developed

contact/material layer, instead of rewriting rigid-body simulation from scratch.
6. Inverse physics: learning material properties from touch
The core data-to-world question is whether you can infer physical material parameters from known actions and
measured contact responses. This is a form of inverse mechanics / system identification, distinct from simply learning to
classify textures.
θ* = arg minθ Σa∈A d( Sim(M(θ), a), Treal(a) ) + λ R(θ)
Here θ contains physically meaningful model parameters; A is a set of probing actions; d compares predicted and
observed signals; and R regularizes ambiguous solutions. A differentiable simulator can provide gradients, but even
nondifferentiable systems can be fit through search or surrogate models.
Relevant differentiable physics work
ChainQueen applies differentiable material-point simulation to soft robotics [13]. DiffTaichi develops differentiable
programming for physical simulation [14]. PlasticineLab uses differentiable mechanics for deformable-body
manipulation [15]. Dojo investigates differentiable robot contact/friction and system identification [16]. IPC and related
work improve robust treatment of deformable-body contact [17]. None alone solves tactile material identification, but
they supply mathematical infrastructure for it.
Identifiability is a major open issue
The same force trace may be reproduced by different combinations of sensor compliance, object compliance, friction
and geometric microstructure. Accurate labels therefore need controlled normal loads, independent force/torque
measurement, known motion, calibration specimens, repeat measurements, and multiple action types. A model that fits
seen pressing trajectories may still fail under new sliding speeds. Sensor aging, fabrication variability, temperature and
wear must be separated from material properties.
Hybrid simulation plus learned residual
An effective architecture may use: (i) a physical material/contact model for causally meaningful pressure and shear; (ii)
an observation model for a particular sensor; and (iii) a learned residual to capture microtexture, optical effects,
unmodeled vibration or solver bias. In abstract form:
Tpred = Render( Contact(θ, a) ) + ResidualML(history, a)
The residual should remain limited and tested out of distribution, otherwise the model risks reverting to a black-box
texture predictor. An uncertainty estimate should flag conditions beyond the calibrated loading, speed, sensor or
environmental range.

7. Multisensory object representations: ObjectFolder and
related work
ObjectFolder is among the closest precursors to the idea of a downloadable touchable object. Its implicit
representations let virtual objects produce visual, auditory and tactile observations; ObjectFolder 2.0 scales the concept
to 1,000 common objects and studies sim-to-real transfer [18] [19].
The crucial architectural difference in the proposed project is to distinguish a physical object field from a
sensor-dependent tactile response. An object-specific tactile network can generate the appropriate GelSight image
without necessarily determining a stable set of coefficients governing friction, wear, compliance or force under arbitrary
new motions. A more transferable system would separate:
Geometry + intrinsic material field → contact mechanics → canonical physical contact state → sensor renderer →
observed tactile stream.
This structure could allow a new tactile sensor, body skin or robot to interact with the same material asset. It also
enables benchmarks for each link in the chain rather than evaluating only the final image. Vision-touch cross-modal

8. Existing tactile world models: what they do and how
The five models discussed in our October conversation establish that learned tactile prediction already exists. They are
not equivalent to a complete virtual physical world. Most learn regularities from recorded interaction trajectories rather
than reconstructing the full underlying microscale material mechanics.
The generic training and usage loop
• Collect synchronized demonstrations of pushing, grasping, sliding, insertion or wiping with cameras, tactile sensors,
motor actions, robot state and often wrist force/torque.
• Encode tactile images or taxels into a lower-dimensional latent representation; encode video, proprioception and
candidate actions.
• Predict future tactile signals, latent states, visual frames, contact events or slip across a limited horizon.
• Act by planning against these futures, detecting impending instability, or synthesizing recovery examples for policy
learning.

For intuition, consider a robot holding a glass: it senses the current grasp, imagines loosening its fingers, forecasts
falling contact pressure and possible slip, and tightens its grip. This is predictive sensor modeling even if the system
never explicitly estimates the glass friction coefficient.
SPOTS — predicting touch and vision together
What: SPOTS jointly predicts future visual and tactile frames from robot-pushing trajectories. It uses modality-specific
pathways with cross-modal interaction. Why: touch adds information especially when objects look the same but differ
physically. Boundary: the experiments involve pushing and particular tactile measurements, not arbitrary materials or
whole-body touch [39].

Sparsh
Sparsh learns broad vision-based tactile features from unlabeled touch images and evaluates these representations on
tactile tasks. Its importance is methodological: tactile perception can be pretrained rather than learned from scratch for
each grasp or surface. However, good downstream scores do not imply the learned embedding corresponds one-to-one
with intrinsic physical coefficients [27].
AnyTouch and AnyTouch 2
AnyTouch aligns tactile representations across heterogeneous visuotactile sensors, suggesting that a common latent
space could allow transfer across hardware. AnyTouch 2 extends the program toward temporal/contact dynamics and
force-aware information [28] [29]. A sensor-agnostic latent is valuable, but it is not automatically a sensor-independent
physical contact field. The proposed architecture should keep those concepts separate.
Connecting Touch and Vision
Cross-modal prediction research shows visual cues can predict tactile-related information and tactile cues can inform
visual inference [26]. This supports a multisensory world model with a shared physical latent state, but
action-conditioned validation remains essential: recognizing that a cloth is rough is not the same as predicting how
force and vibration change during a new stroke.

10. Human-like whole-body tactile sensing
Human somatosensation goes beyond fingertip contact. Skin conveys pressure, motion, vibration, stretch, warmth and
cold; the body simultaneously integrates proprioception and motor predictions. A robot approximating human-scale
touch needs distributed sensors, calibration, skin geometry, bandwidth management and a body-aware
perception/control system.
What a full-body state might contain
Skin(s,t) = [ P, Fx, Fy, Fz, shear, slip, vibration, temperature, deformation ]
Here s indexes points on the robot’s physical body surface. No existing affordable skin necessarily measures all
components directly at high resolution. Some must be estimated from nearby measurements or from a mechanistic skin
model. The target is a distributed sensing and state-estimation system, not simply a dense matrix of force values.
Whole-body humanoid artificial skin
A self-organizing modular multimodal skin has been demonstrated on a humanoid body in earlier research [30]. Newer
whole-body tactile-control work uses distributed pressure/force, proximity, vibration or temperature readings to inform
compliant contact behavior [31]. These show that whole-body tactile sensing and control are active research areas,
though they do not provide a universal physics-calibrated texture world model.
Scalable electronic skin: RobotSweater
RobotSweater develops a customizable knitted pressure-sensitive skin that can cover curved and large areas and
detect/localize contact [32]. Its relevance is scalability: a humanoid cannot afford an optical camera and elastomer
assembly beneath every square centimeter. Lower-resolution large-area pressure skin may coexist with dense,
high-bandwidth fingertips.
ReSkin, AnySkin and D’Manus
ReSkin studies flexible magnetic tactile sensing with replaceable sensing surfaces [35]. AnySkin explores a practical
plug-and-play direction and transfer across replacement skins [36]. D’Manus distributes magnetic sensing across the
fingers and palm and offers relevant integrated hand/simulation ideas [37]. These works motivate a central
standardization problem: signals from two skins may differ because of manufacturing, electronics or calibration even on
the same object.
A realistic hierarchical body design
• Fingertips: high-spatial-resolution normal/shear contact, microslip, vibration and local deformation.
• Palms / feet: broad contact patches, load distribution, shear direction and stability.
• Arms / torso / legs: coarser large-area pressure, proximity and protective contact sensing, with local higher-resolution
zones where needed.
• Central representation: map every measurement to registered body geometry, time, calibration metadata and an
uncertainty estimate.
This yields heterogeneous resolution, which is likely more practical than identical “human-like” sensing everywhere. It
also introduces a modeling challenge: sensors across the body must have a shared coordinate system and comparable
units even when their sensing physics differs.

Sensor and object diversity: do not confuse variation sources
To measure transfer, vary materials, object exemplars, sensor units and fabrication batches deliberately. More objects
are not a substitute for multiple replacement sensors; multiple sensors are not a substitute for unseen sliding speeds or
normal loads. ReSkin/AnySkin motivates explicit sensor-interchange experiments, and the discussion noted that learned
transfer can still leave physical-scale force bias. Consequently any self-supervised alignment should be supplemented
by independently measured force labels before it is interpreted as metrological calibration [35] [36].

World/skin decoupling is essential
Keep the object’s intrinsic properties separate from the sensor response. Do not train one object→GelSight image
network and call that a sensor-independent material model. The most portable interface is a documented contact field
with spatial coordinates, standard units, time resolution, numerical tolerances and calibration assumptions.
Two fidelity modes
• Fast training mode: approximate contact patches, learned vibration residuals, GPU-parallel sensing, coarse body taxels.
Optimized for large-scale policy rollouts.
• Accurate validation mode: higher-resolution microgeometry, nonlinear contact, FEM reference simulations, higher
sampling rates and detailed sensor calibration. Optimized for measuring error and identifying parameters.
This split is a practical way to reconcile a world-model training loop with the physical complexity of fine texture. A
full-FEM, whole-body millisecond-by-millisecond world may be computationally prohibitive; the model should spend
precision where it affects action.

A causal physical state vs. a latent state
A latent z_touch can be excellent for predicting slip while encoding friction and deformation in uninterpretable
combinations. A physical state provides units and more explicit counterfactuals but may miss sensor artifacts or
complex tribology. The strongest strategy may be to retain both: physical parameters for intervention and an
expressive latent for residual dynamics. The models must be independently evaluated against held-out actions and
samples.

a broadly validated, reusable stack that combines intrinsic tactile material assets, realistic dynamic contact, multiple
sensor modalities, and high-coverage body sensing with action-conditioned planning.

What to avoid claiming
• Do not claim existing physics engines model no friction; they generally do at several degrees of fidelity.
• Do not equate recognition embeddings, sensor image synthesis, contact mechanics and whole-body somatosensation.
• Do not claim a learned predictor “understands material” merely because it forecasts one familiar motion.
• Do not equate cross-sensor feature alignment with physical force calibration.
• Do not interpret one paper’s task score as directly comparable to another paper’s success rate.
• Do not claim no one has explored tactile world models: SPOTS, TacForeSight, TACO, FeelWorld and ContactWorld are
direct counterexamples.

14. A feasible first research study: calibrated tactile material
transfer
A strong first paper or startup prototype is not a full-body human-touch simulator. It is a controlled demonstration that a
learned physical surface model predicts tactile behavior under new actions better than existing alternatives. The study
needs reproducible measurements, baselines and a clear failure analysis.
Research question and hypothesis
Question: Can a reusable, physically parameterized tactile material representation learned from a small set of
controlled interactions accurately predict force, vibration, slip and tactile observations under previously unseen contact
conditions?
Hypothesis: With equal training data, a hybrid physics+residual model will generalize more robustly across unseen
normal forces, scan speeds, directions and sensor instances than a sensor-specific black-box predictor.
Experimental setup
• Materials: 3-5 well-characterized surfaces with contrasting physics: smooth polymer/glass-like surface, silicone rubber,
denim or woven fabric, corrugated cardboard and brushed metal. Use repeat specimens.
• Actuator: robot arm or controlled linear stage with a repeatable probe trajectory; log commanded and measured
motion.
• Sensors: one tactile fingertip/probe plus independent force/torque measurement; if possible a high-sample-rate
vibration sensor and visible markers for motion tracking.
• Trials: normal indentation and release, speed-controlled sliding, multiple normal loads, two or more scan directions; add
taps/twists only after a reliable baseline.
• Synchronization: all streams on a checked time base with known frame/sample rates; archive raw calibration and
contact geometry.

Metrics and failure analysis
Report calibrated normal/shear force error; contact-area or pressure-field error; slip-onset timing and detection;
frequency-domain vibration similarity; temporal prediction robustness; sensor transfer/calibration cost; and real
downstream grasp, push or insertion success. Include per-material and per-held-out-condition breakdowns, error bars
across repeated trials, and ablations removing microgeometry, friction, physical supervision or learned residuals.
Milestones, in sequence
• Phase 1 - Measurement: get one probe, one force reference and one material to produce repeatable press/slide traces;
verify calibration.
• Phase 2 - Contact baseline: fit a simple friction/compliance model and predict an untouched trial; characterize the
dominant errors.
• Phase 3 - Material comparison: expand to 3-5 surfaces and controlled ranges of speed, load and direction.
• Phase 4 - Learning baselines: compare black-box predictor, pure physics and hybrid residual under identical held-out
tests.
• Phase 5 - Transfer: test a new specimen/sensor instance and demonstrate improvement in at least one robot task.
• Phase 6 - Reuse: export machine-readable material assets and connect them to a simulator independent of the original
hardware.
Publication target: a falsifiable contribution to tactile representation and inverse physical modeling. A credible negative
result that explains non-identifiability or cross-sensor failure can also be scientifically meaningful.

What could be commercially defensible?
The central value proposition is not collecting enormous unstructured touch recordings. It is turning well-measured
physical interactions into reusable, validated simulation-ready tactile material assets, with calibration procedures,
provenance and known generalization bounds. A secondary offering could be standardized sensor data adapters and
tactile simulation/robot-learning benchmarks. A third offering, later, could be a broad multimodal world model.

Research vs. business claims
A material database is only useful to customers if synthetic tactile training demonstrably improves a real manipulation
outcome (success rate, drops, excess force, tolerance to object variation or adaptation cost). This requires controlled
tests against comparable vision-only, force-only and raw-tactile baselines. Market value, exclusivity, demand and
willingness to pay were not established by the papers discussed; they need direct customer validation.
Best positioning for PhD research
A research program should emphasize an identifiable, testable scientific question rather than only a product vision. A
strong first submission would include a dataset with rigorous ground truth, a physically interpretable model or a new
benchmark, controlled ablations and held-out transfer tests. Whole-body tactile intelligence then becomes a coherent
long-term research arc connecting contact physics, machine perception and robot learning.
Open questions raised by the conversation
• How many independent physical parameters can touch actually identify, and under what action diversity?
• Is microgeometry essential, or can a low-dimensional speed/load-dependent response model suffice for robot policy
training?
• Which physical quantities must be modeled explicitly, and which can be learned as a residual?
• How does calibration error vary across tactile skins, fabrication batches, days and sensor types?
• What is the cheapest sensing protocol that achieves reliable cross-action transfer?
• Can a contact-field representation reproduce different sensors without per-object retraining?
• Does pressure/shear/vibration fidelity lead to measurable success on unfamiliar objects, or are simpler tactile cues
sufficient?
• How should a humanoid allocate sensing density across fingertips, palms, feet and torso?

18. Compact technical glossary and assumptions
SDF. Signed distance field: a representation of shape and proximity to a surface.
Taxel. A tactile sensing element, analogous to a pixel but measuring contact-related quantities.
Normal force / pressure. Force perpendicular to the surface; pressure expresses force per area.
Shear traction. Tangential force per area across a contact patch; essential for slip and friction.
Incipient slip. Partial local slipping before an object undergoes macroscopic sliding.
Compliance. Degree of deformation under applied load; depends on geometry and material.
Viscoelasticity. Time/rate-dependent mechanical response that mixes elastic and dissipative behavior.
Hydroelastic contact. Contact representation with spatially distributed contact surface/pressure.
FEM. Finite-element method for approximating continuum deformation and stress.
MPM. Material point method for simulating deformable bodies with particle/grid representations.
Inverse physics / system identification. Inferring material or model parameters from observed interactions.
Latent tactile state. Learned compressed representation of tactile measurements.
Sim-to-real. Testing whether a policy/model trained or calibrated in simulation works on physical hardware.
Provenance. Record of where measurements came from, including sensor IDs, calibration and specimen information.
Important assumptions: Equations and asset schemas in this report are conceptual, not an established universal tactile standard.
The label “somatosensory world model” expresses the long-term research goal. The idea that a hybrid physics-based model
improves transfer is a proposed experimental hypothesis. Individual published metrics are quoted only within their original
experimental scopes. An absence of a fully integrated system in the reviewed works does not prove that no undiscovered project
exists.
Final perspective. The opportunity is to connect three presently separated advances:
physics-based contact, real measured tactile material properties, and learned future
tactile prediction. A usable first milestone is a verified material/contact benchmark.
Full-body synthetic touch should be treated as the ultimate systems challenge, pursued
once transfer across actions and sensors is credible.
